Abstract

Electrified aircraft and unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) place heat-generating electronics inside carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer (CFRP) airframes whose through-thickness thermal conductivity is low, while the outer skin absorbs solar radiation. This capstone project develops asymmetric aerogel interleaves for CFRP laminates: a boron nitride (BN)-rich freeze-cast surface layer to reflect sunlight and insulate, and carbon-fabric plies treated with a carbon-nanotube (CNT)-rich aerogel to spread internal heat. Six four-ply configurations (L1–L5 and a plain reference P) are compared under internal heating, simulated sunlight and both together. Because the conductivity of a freeze-cast aerogel depends on its pore structure and wall composition in ways that are slow to map experimentally, a simulation strand was set up to predict the conductivity of the aerogel layer along and across its channels from its structure and its recipe.

Two tools were built. The first, a browser-based simulator, decomposes the conductivity into solid, gas, radiative and convective paths; each path has a closed-form method and a pixel-based detailed method that must reproduce the formula in the ideal case before it is trusted. The second, a GPU-ready voxel solver, computes the wall conductivity from the recipe and then solves the honeycomb at its true wall thickness. At the reference geometry (30 µm pores, 0.27 µm walls, wall conductivity 4 W/m·K) the model gives 61.3 and 96.8 mW/m·K across and along the channels (anisotropy 1.58); cracks in 5 % of each wall lower the transverse value by 23 %. All 53 unit tests and 11 built-in solver checks pass, including an independent finite-element benchmark matched to within 0.35 %.

Three discrepancies with published measurements are identified and analysed: transverse values of BNNS/polymer aerogels below the air-only floor of an air-filled micron-scale honeycomb, a measured anisotropy of 5.8 for BNNS/polyurethane against a modelled 1.6–2.2 (a CNF/BN/MTMS cryogel of the project's own constituent family shows 1.83, inside the modelled range), and an idealised BN-rich wall predicted to conduct about 15 times better than the value back-calculated from the literature. A first sensitivity study ranks sheet-to-sheet contact resistance, BN in-plane conductivity, sheet alignment and wall porosity as the inputs whose measurement would most reduce the uncertainty. Semester two will model the six laminate specimens, calibrated once on the plain reference, and propagate uncertainty through the chain.

Keywords: freeze-cast aerogel; boron nitride; anisotropic thermal conductivity; finite volume; homogenisation; GPU computing; CFRP thermal management

1 Introduction

1.1 Background

Electrified propulsion is moving heat sources into places where aircraft structures were never designed to carry them. Battery packs, motor controllers and power electronics in UAVs and electric aircraft sit inside thin composite skins, and every kilogram of active cooling hardware costs range. Carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer (CFRP) is the natural airframe material for these vehicles because of its specific stiffness and strength, but its polymer matrix makes it a poor conductor through its thickness: carbon/epoxy laminates conduct well below 1 W/m·K across the plies and several times more along the fibres (Villière et al., 2013). Heat generated inside the laminate therefore accumulates locally, while the outer skin, exposed to sunlight of up to about 1000 W/m², absorbs an additional load from outside.

Porous, highly reflective materials offer a passive answer to the outer half of this problem. Radiative-cooling research has shown that surfaces reflecting most of the solar spectrum can stay below ambient temperature in direct sunlight (Raman et al., 2014), and freeze-cast aerogels built from boron nitride nanosheets (BNNS) combine a solar reflectance above 90 % with a thermal conductivity at or below that of still air (Chan et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022; Adegun et al., 2023). The inner half of the problem asks for the opposite property: a layer that spreads heat sideways so that it can leave the laminate over a larger area. Carbon nanotubes (CNT) and other carbon fillers are the usual route to such conductive networks.

This project combines both ideas in one laminate (Figure 1.1). A BN-rich freeze-cast layer on the outer surface reflects sunlight and insulates; T300 carbon-fabric plies treated with a CNT-rich CNF/CNT/MTMS slurry redistribute internal heat; both are integrated into a four-ply laminate by vacuum-assisted resin infusion (VARI). Six configurations isolate the effect of aerogel location, the BN surface layer and the type of surface coating (Table 3.4).

1.2 Problem statement

The experimental chain from slurry to tested laminate is long: mixing, freeze casting, freeze drying, resin infusion, curing, conditioning and testing take days per batch, and the planned programme can vary only a handful of parameters. Yet the conductivity of an aerogel layer depends on many of them at once: pore size and wall thickness, the alignment of the channels relative to the heat flow, the composition and microstructure of the walls, the gas in the pores, and whether resin has entered the layer during infusion. Without a model, the measured temperature of a laminate cannot be traced back to any one of these causes, and the next formulation has to be chosen by trial.

Three features make the modelling problem harder than it first appears. First, the walls of a freeze-cast aerogel are about 0.27 µm thick while the pores are about 30 µm wide (Chan et al., 2022): two orders of magnitude apart, so a single numerical grid cannot resolve both cheaply. Second, the conductivity of the wall material itself, a mixture of BN sheets, cellulose nanofibrils (CNF) and a silsesquioxane binder, is not known and cannot be looked up. Third, several published aerogels report transverse conductivities below that of still air, which an honest model of air-filled micron-scale channels cannot reproduce. A useful simulation must therefore be transparent about its inputs and report gaps rather than hide them by tuning.

1.3 Aim and objectives

The aim of the project is to develop and experimentally evaluate a thermo-optically functional CFRP laminate that combines internal heat-management capability with external solar protection. Its objectives are:

  1. O1 To design and fabricate CNF/CNT/MTMS and CNF/BN/MTMS layers by freeze casting and VARI in different laminate configurations.
  2. O2 To investigate the influence of CNT and BN aerogel content on the thermal behaviour, spectral reflectance and mechanical strength of the CFRP laminate.
  3. O3 To integrate selected formulations into CFRP by VARI and compare them with conventional CFRP under internal heating, simulated solar irradiation and simultaneous loading.

The simulation strand reported here supports O2 and O3 with four objectives of its own:

  1. S1 To build a transparent, verified model of the thermal conductivity of a dry freeze-cast honeycomb aerogel along and across its channels, decomposed by heat path.
  2. S2 To link the slurry recipe (BN : CNF : MTMS) to the conductivity of the aerogel wall, reported as a band with the reason for its width.
  3. S3 To identify which uncertain inputs dominate the predicted conductivity, and therefore which measurements are worth making first.
  4. S4 To predict the temperature rise of the six laminate specimens (L1–L5 and the plain reference P) under the planned load cases, calibrating once on P only (semester two).

2 Literature Review

The review is organised around the questions the simulation has to answer rather than paper by paper: how heat moves through a highly porous insulator (Section 2.2); what anisotropic freeze-cast aerogels actually measure, and how far those measurements can be trusted (Section 2.3); what sets the conductivity of the thin solid walls (Section 2.4); and how other fields compute effective properties from a picture of the structure (Section 2.5). Section 2.1 places the work in its application, and Section 2.6 states the gap this project addresses.

2.1 Thermal management of CFRP and the case for thermo-optical interleaves

The anisotropy of CFRP is the starting point. Villière et al. (2013) measured and modelled the full conductivity tensor of a carbon/epoxy composite and showed that the transverse conductivity is governed by the epoxy and the fibre packing, remaining well below 1 W/m·K, while the fibre direction conducts several times better. For an electrified airframe this means that heat from an internal source spreads poorly through the skin, and any added layer acts in series with a resistive laminate. The same anisotropy appears again in the aerogel layers of this project, so the simulation must report a conductivity tensor, not a single number.

On the outer surface the relevant literature is that of daytime radiative cooling. Raman et al. (2014) demonstrated sub-ambient cooling under direct sunlight using a surface that reflects most of the solar spectrum and emits in the atmospheric window. Boron nitride is attractive here because it is white, electrically insulating and thermally stable, and BNNS-based aerogels reach solar reflectances of 94–97 % (Chan et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022; Adegun et al., 2023). Those studies target buildings; this project transfers the idea to a structural laminate, where the aerogel must also survive resin infusion. That shift matters for the model: an aerogel whose pores fill with epoxy is no longer an insulator (Section 4.5).

2.2 Heat transfer in highly porous insulators

The standard description of heat flow in aerogels and foams treats it as several parallel paths: conduction through the solid skeleton, conduction through the gas in the pores, thermal radiation, and convection (Figure 2.1). Hrubesh and Pekala (1994) used this split to explain why organic and inorganic aerogels reach conductivities below still air, and it remains the organising framework of recent reviews (Lou et al., 2023; Apostolopoulou-Kalkavoura et al., 2021). Obori et al. (2019) applied it quantitatively to cellulose-nanofibril aerogels: a simple open-cell model for the solid, the Kaganer expression for the gas and a radiative term were fitted to measurements at different solid fractions, and the fitted components were used to identify the structure that would minimise the total conductivity. That study is the closest methodological precedent to the present work, with one important difference: it fits the solid contribution to data, whereas this project tries to predict it from the wall composition.

Gas conduction. When the pore size approaches the mean free path of the gas molecules (about 66 nm for air at 300 K and 1 atm), molecules collide with the walls more often than with each other and the gas conducts less. Kaganer (1969) gave the widely used interpolation between the continuum and free-molecular limits, and Zeng et al. (1995) showed how the effective mean free path in a porous solid follows from its surface-to-volume ratio. The same physics underpins vacuum insulation panels, whose conductivity rises steeply once the pressure is high enough for the gas mean free path to fall below the pore size (Baetens et al., 2010). For 30 µm channels at atmospheric pressure the Knudsen reduction is below 1 %; it becomes important only at low pressure or if the walls themselves contain nanopores, a possibility that returns in Section 4.6.

Radiation. In cellular materials radiation is usually modelled either as exchange between opaque cell walls or, when the material is optically thick, as a diffusion process with the Rosseland mean extinction coefficient (Glicksman, 1994; Modest, 2013). Randrianalisoa and Baillis (2014) reviewed both families for solid foams and identified Monte Carlo ray tracing on the real geometry as the most general way to obtain the extinction coefficient. The key limitation is the input: radiative properties of thin BN/CNF walls in the thermal infrared are not reported, so any radiative term in this project is an order-of-magnitude estimate until they are measured.

Convection. Natural convection inside closed cells starts only when the Rayleigh number of the cell exceeds about 1708. Early work on foamed plastics found it negligible for cells smaller than a few millimetres (Skochdopole, 1961), and most foam models omit it for that reason (Collishaw & Evans, 1994). Freeze-cast pores are three orders of magnitude smaller, so convection can be shown to be absent rather than modelled.

Critical assessment. The additive split is a modelling convenience, not a law. Where gas and solid interact at the wall, for example at nanoscale contacts that air can bridge, the paths couple. Han et al. (2022) resolved this coupling in nanoporous materials with a lattice Boltzmann model and showed that it can change the effective conductivity appreciably at the nanoscale. For micron-scale channels the coupling is expected to be small, but a model built on the split should either resolve the solid and gas together where it can, or state that it does not. Both are done in this work (Section 3.2.3).

2.3 Anisotropic freeze-cast aerogels: structure and measured conductivity

Freeze casting freezes a slurry under a temperature gradient so that ice crystals grow along the gradient and push the suspended solids into walls between them; sublimation of the ice leaves aligned channels. Scotti and Dunand (2018) compiled processing and structure data for hundreds of freeze-cast materials and showed that pore size falls as the freezing front velocity rises, while wall thickness and porosity follow the solids loading. For the simulation this establishes the variables that link processing to structure (pore size, wall thickness, alignment) and justifies treating structure, not processing, as the model input.

The aligned channels make the conductivity anisotropic: heat travels more easily along the walls than across them. Wicklein et al. (2015) reported nanocellulose/graphene-oxide foams with a radial conductivity of about 15 mW/m·K, well below still air. In the supervisor's group, Chan et al. (2022) developed an additive freeze-casting process for BNNS/waterborne polyurethane (WPU) panels with in-plane channels of about 30 µm, a porosity of 98.2 % and a density of 20.2 mg/cm³; the out-of-plane conductivity was 16.9 mW/m·K, the anisotropy factor 5.8 and the solar reflectance 97 %. Kim et al. (2023) showed that lowering the freezing temperature produces a more anisotropic BNNS/WPU structure with 16.2 mW/m·K, and proposed a theoretical approach to estimate the anisotropic conductivity of such porous materials. BNNS/polyvinyl alcohol aerogels from the same group reached 23.5 mW/m·K (Yang et al., 2022) and 20.3 mW/m·K (Adegun et al., 2023) through the thickness. Closest to this project, Zhao et al. (2022) freeze-cast a CNF/hydroxylated-BNNS/MTMS cryogel on liquid nitrogen, the same constituents as the B-series, and reported 62.1 and 33.9 mW/m·K axially and radially (anisotropy 1.83) together with radiative-cooling capability. Critical assessment. Three points in this body of work matter for modelling. First, every BNNS/polymer value in Table 2.1 is at or below the conductivity of still air (26.3 mW/m·K at 300 K; (Bergman et al., 2011)). In an air-filled honeycomb with 30 µm channels and a porosity of 0.98, the gas alone should carry about φ·kair ≈ 25.6 mW/m·K, so a measured total of 16.9 mW/m·K implies either that the gas conduction is suppressed by pores much smaller than the channels (for example nanopores in the walls), or that the measurement underestimates the transverse value. Chan et al. used a transient plane source (Hot Disk) instrument (Gustafsson, 1991); on strongly anisotropic, highly porous samples the result depends on the analysis model and on the assumed heat capacity, a sensitivity discussed in the test-method review of Lou et al. (2023). A model that matched 16.9 mW/m·K with 30 µm air channels would therefore be matching the wrong thing. The CNF/BNNS/MTMS cryogel of Zhao et al. (2022) is the exception: its radial value lies above the air floor, as an air-filled channel model requires.

Second, the porosity in Chan et al. is defined as P = 1 − ρ/ρ0, with ρ0 the weighted average of the WPU (1.02 g/cm³) and BNNS (2.25 g/cm³) densities. With ρ = 20.2 mg/cm³ and P = 98.2 %, ρ0 = 1.12 g/cm³, which corresponds to a wall of only about 8–16 wt% BNNS depending on how the average is formed. The wall of that aerogel is therefore polymer-rich, a fact that changes how its conductivity can be used as a reference for the BN-rich walls of this project (Section 4.6). This reading of the paper should be confirmed with its authors. Third, none of these studies provides a predictive model that starts from the recipe: the theoretical approach of Kim et al. (2023) is the closest and is the natural benchmark for this work.

2.4 What sets the conductivity of the wall

For a thin-walled cellular solid the solid contribution scales with the solid fraction and with a geometric factor that accounts for walls inclined to the heat flow. For three-dimensional foams with thin struts the factor is about one third (Collishaw & Evans, 1994; Gibson & Ashby, 1997); for a two-dimensional hexagonal honeycomb heated across its channels the equivalent factor is one half (derived in Section 3.2.3). Rigorous bounds on any two-phase mixture are given by Wiener (parallel and series) and, for isotropic arrangements, by Hashin and Shtrikman (1962); Torquato (2002) reviews both. These bounds bracket the honeycomb result and are used as checks throughout this work.

The open question is the conductivity of the wall material itself. Bulk hexagonal BN conducts about 585 W/m·K in-plane when isotopically pure and about 325 W/m·K with the natural isotope mixture, but only about 5 W/m·K through its basal planes (Yuan et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2018). In composites the effective value is controlled by how the sheets touch. Figure 2.2 shows the spread: BN/nanofibrillated-cellulose papers reach 145.7 W/m·K at 50 wt% BN (Zhu et al., 2014), CNF/BNNS films 82.4 W/m·K at 90 % BNNS (Kong et al., 2022), while BNNS/CNF nano-papers at the same 50 wt% reach only 2.4 W/m·K, or 1.2 W/m·K when simply blended (Wang et al., 2019). The same nominal composition spans two orders of magnitude.

Effective-medium theory explains part of this spread. Nan et al. (1997) extended Maxwell-type models to aligned ellipsoidal particles with an interfacial thermal resistance, the Kapitza resistance reviewed by Swartz and Pohl (1989), and showed that for thin platelets the interface term can dominate. These models assume a dilute filler in a continuous matrix. The recipes of this project, however, produce walls that are 62–91 vol% BN (Section 3.2.3): BN is the continuous phase and the binder is a thin glue between sheets. In that regime heat must cross sheet-to-sheet contacts whose resistance depends on overlap, alignment and binder thickness, and no closed-form model captures this reliably. This is the main reason the project builds an explicit voxel model of the wall, and why its deliverable is a band of wall conductivities rather than one number.

2.5 Computing effective properties from structure

Computing the conductivity of a heterogeneous material from a picture of it is a mature problem in several industries. The common method is homogenisation on a periodic representative volume element (RVE): impose a mean temperature gradient, solve the steady conduction equation with periodic boundary conditions and average the heat flux (Torquato, 2002). NASA's Porous Microstructure Analysis software (PuMA) applies this to micro-tomography of carbon-fibre preforms for spacecraft heat shields (Ferguson et al., 2018); TauFactor does the same for battery electrodes, and its second version runs on GPUs (Cooper et al., 2016; Kench et al., 2023). FFT-based homogenisation, introduced by Moulinec and Suquet (1998) for composites, exploits periodicity so that a uniform reference medium can be inverted exactly in Fourier space; the GPU solver of this project uses that idea as a preconditioner. For pore-size statistics, the local-thickness transform of Hildebrand and Rüegsegger (1997), developed for bone micro-CT and implemented in PoreSpy (Gostick et al., 2019), gives the size of the largest sphere that fits at every point.

For the other paths, lattice Boltzmann methods reproduce the onset of Rayleigh–Bénard convection (Shan, 1997; Mohamad, 2011), and direct simulation Monte Carlo resolves rarefied gas flow (Bird, 1994). When structure images are scarce, SliceGAN generates statistically equivalent 3-D volumes from a single 2-D micrograph (Kench & Cooper, 2021). Finally, the verification and validation literature (Oberkampf & Roy, 2010; ASME, 2009) supplies the discipline that ties these tools together: each numerical model is first shown to solve its equations correctly on problems with known answers, and only then compared with experiments. The project therefore writes its own small solvers and uses the published tools as references for method and, optionally, as independent checks.

2.6 Research gap and positioning

The review identifies three gaps. (i) Measured transverse conductivities of anisotropic BNNS/polymer aerogels lie at or below the air-only floor of their stated structures, and the literature does not separate the contributions that would explain this. (ii) No predictive link exists from a BN : CNF : MTMS recipe to wall conductivity; mixture models are applied outside their validity range, and measured films of similar composition span two orders of magnitude. (iii) Effective-property tools exist, but not as a transparent chain from recipe to laminate that states the status of every input.

This project positions itself accordingly. It does not aim to fit published conductivities; it aims to predict trends from structure, to compute the wall conductivity from its constituents with an honest uncertainty band, and to report where the model and the measurements disagree and why. The theoretical estimate of Kim et al. (2023) and the component model of Obori et al. (2019) are the benchmarks against which its usefulness will be judged.

3 Methodology

3.1 Overall approach

The project follows a processing–structure–property framework. Processing parameters (recipe, freezing conditions, infusion) produce a structure (pore size, wall thickness, alignment, wall microstructure), and the structure produces the properties (conductivity, reflectance, strength). The experiments span the whole chain from processing to properties; the simulation covers the step from structure to properties, where cause and effect can be separated one variable at a time; the literature anchors both.

The supervisor's guidance at the project meeting fixed the first simulation study: model the aerogel on its own before the laminate, use a simple idealised geometry, hold one composition fixed and vary the pore size, compute the conductivity both along and across the alignment, and compare trends with measurements rather than absolute values. Porosity and density are to follow from the geometry, not be set independently. These constraints shape every choice in Section 3.2.

The simulation work is split between two pieces of software, kept apart on purpose. The aerosim web tool explains each heat path with a fast formula and a detailed pixel method and runs on any laptop with NumPy and SciPy. The K_cond solver computes the one number the web tool cannot derive, the wall conductivity for the real recipe, together with the honeycomb at its true wall thickness; it needs heavy computation and is written to run on a GPU in Google Colab. Its result feeds back into the web tool as the wall conductivity.

3.2 Simulation methodology

3.2.1 Scope, strategy and modelling chain

The object of the first study is the dry freeze-cast skeleton: thin solid walls around hexagonal channels of air aligned with the freezing direction. Carbon fibre, resin and the laminate are excluded at this stage. Figure 3.1 shows the modelling chain from recipe and geometry to the layer conductivity and, in semester two, to the specimens. The conductivity is decomposed into four heat paths, evaluated separately along (∥) and across (⊥) the channels:

ktotal = ksolid + kgas + krad + kconv,    anisotropy = ktotal,∥ / ktotal,⊥(3.1)

Four rules govern the implementation. (1) Each heat path is counted once. A method that resolves two paths together, such as the pixel finite-volume method that solves solid and gas in one picture, declares this, and the other row is not added again. (2) Two levels per path. Every path has a fast closed-form method from the literature and a detailed method that works on a pixel picture of the structure. (3) Reproduce first. A detailed method is trusted where the formula cannot go (cracks, uneven cells, narrow gaps) only after it reproduces the formula in the ideal case; this is enforced by automated tests. (4) Every input carries its provenance: a value, a unit, a source and a status (measured, literature, derived or hypothetical). No input is tuned to match a measurement; gaps are reported. Methods that were considered and rejected are listed in Table 3.1.

A documented shortcut. Real walls are only t/d ≈ 0.009 of the cell; resolving them with four pixels would need about 400 pixels per cell. The web tool instead draws walls 4 pixels wide at 100 pixels per cell and gives each wall pixel a solid fraction s = t/tdraw, the rest being air side by side. Along the wall the two conduct in parallel, so the wall conductance t·kwall is preserved exactly. Four pixels rather than two are needed because a 2-pixel band at ±30° on a square grid becomes a thin staircase that carries only 64 % of its true heat; at 4 pixels the honeycomb result is within 3 %. Porosity is counted from the picture (φ = 0.9825 against 0.9822 analytically). The GPU solver removes this shortcut altogether (Rung 3 below).

3.2.3 Solid conduction: a fidelity ladder

The solid path carries most of the heat (58 % across and 73 % along the channels at the defaults; Section 4.2) and depends on the least certain input, so it receives four levels of treatment (Figure 3.3). Each rung is checked against the one below it before it is used where the lower rung cannot go.

Rung 1: closed form and bounds. Along the channels the walls are straight strips parallel to the heat flow, in parallel with the air, so the solid term is exactly (1 − φ)kwall. Across the channels a thin wall at angle θ to the mean temperature gradient carries heat only along itself: it sees the component |∇T| cos θ of the gradient, and only the component cos θ of its heat flow lies in the flow direction (Figure 3.4). Each wall therefore contributes in proportion to cos²θ. A hexagonal network has walls at 0°, 60° and 120°, whose mean cos²θ is (1 + ¼ + ¼)/3 = ½. Hence

ksolid,∥ = (1 − φ) kwall,    ksolid,⊥ = ½ (1 − φ) kwall(3.3)

the two-dimensional analogue of the one-third factor for thin-strut foams (Collishaw & Evans, 1994). Because a two-dimensional conductivity tensor that is unchanged by a 120° rotation must be isotropic, the hexagonal honeycomb has a single k⊥ in every in-plane direction; any difference between the x and y values in a calculation is a discretisation error, not physics. The result is bracketed by the two-dimensional Hashin–Shtrikman bounds (Hashin & Shtrikman, 1962), which for wall (w) and fill (h) phases with area fractions f read

kHS± = k1 + f2 / [ 1/(k2 − k1) + f1/(2k1) ],   k1 = max or min of (kw, kh)(3.4)

A naive alternative, slicing the cell into strips in series and parallel, gives an upper bound 22–27 % above the formula at the defaults and is not used as an estimate; its derivation is kept in the repository as a check.

Rung 2: pixel finite volume (web tool). Every pixel of the defects picture is a control volume with conductivity k = s kwall + (1 − s) kgas, where kgas comes from the gas row (a single value, or the local map of Section 3.2.5). The conductance between neighbours is the harmonic mean of two half-pixel resistors in series, and heat balance holds in every pixel (Patankar, 1980):

Σj Gij (Tj − Ti) = 0,    Gij = 2kikj / (ki + kj),    keff = Q L / (A ΔT)(3.5)

The boundary condition is periodic: the picture repeats like wallpaper and the temperature drops by ΔT = 1 K per period, so the link from the last column to the first carries G(Tlast − Tfirst + ΔT). One additional link fixes the arbitrary temperature level. The sparse system is solved directly with SciPy, once along x and once along y, and k⊥ is their mean. This deviates deliberately from the original brief, which proposed fixed temperatures on two sides and insulated edges: on a 4 × 4 block that set-up gave values about 5 % low, and 2 × 2 and 4 × 4 blocks disagreed, because of the cut cells at the edges. With periodic conditions both block sizes give the same answer. Along the channels the pixels act in parallel, so k∥ is the mean pixel conductivity, exactly. Because Rung 2 resolves solid and gas together, including their coupling across the drawn walls, it declares both paths and the gas row is not added again.

Rung 3: honeycomb at true wall thickness (K_cond step 2). The GPU solver draws one periodic unit of two hexagonal cells at the real wall thickness, with 4 (quick mode) or 8 (full mode) pixels across the wall, so the solid-fraction shortcut of Rung 2 is no longer needed. Each pixel face receives its wall area fraction from stratified sub-sampling (4 × 4 samples per edge), which removes most of the staircase error: the x–y split falls from 5.5 % in Rung 2 to 0.16 %. The solve is the same periodic heat balance written in matrix-free form (Section 3.2.4). Through a laminate whose channels are tilted by θ from the plate normal, a wide plate sees the conductivity tensor along its normal:

K = diag(k⊥, k⊥, k∥),   n = (sin θ, 0, cos θ),   kn = nTK n = k∥ cos²θ + k⊥ sin²θ(3.6)

This matters for the experiments: the project's freezing set-up, slurry-treated plies on a metal plate cooled by liquid nitrogen with ice growing upward (Section 3.3), grows channels through the laminate thickness (θ ≈ 0). The specimens' through-thickness value is therefore the layer's high direction, k∥, whereas the BNNS/polymer aerogels of Table 2.1 were tested across in-plane channels. The angle will be confirmed by SEM of the waterjet offcuts.

Rung 4: wall RVE from the recipe (K_cond step 1). The water in the slurry leaves on drying, so only the solids build the walls. From the recipe of the experiment guide (per 50 g batch: BN 0–15 g, 0.50 g dry CNF, 1.00 g MTMS), and assuming that MTMS condenses fully to CH3SiO1.5 (mass yield 67.12/136.22 = 0.493, hypothetical), the dry wall composition follows directly (Figure 3.5). The slurry name is misleading: “B10” has 10 wt% BN in the slurry but builds a wall that is 83 wt% (77 vol%) BN. Every BN-containing group gives a wall of at least 62 vol% BN, so BN is the continuous phase.

The wall is then represented by a periodic three-dimensional voxel box: the true wall thickness (0.27 µm) through z and 1.6–2.5 µm in the wall plane (Figure 3.6). BN sheets are placed as thin discs (default diameter 1 µm, thickness 30 nm, hypothetical) at random centres, tilted from the wall plane by |N(0, 10°)| because the ice front pushes platelets flat, until the target BN volume fraction is reached. Each BN voxel conducts along its sheet's plane and across it (300 and 3 W/m·K; (Yuan et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2018)), the remaining voxels are CNF, PMSQ or pore in proportion to their volume fractions, and every voxel carries the identity of its sheet. Interfaces are handled in the face conductance: a face between two voxels i and j of size h is two half-cells in series with a resistance R,

kf = [ 1/(2ki) + 1/(2kj) + R/h ]−1,   R = RK (BN–binder), Rc (two different sheets), 0 otherwise(3.7)

with RK = 10⁻⁸ m²K/W, the typical order for BN–polymer interfaces (Swartz & Pohl, 1989; Nan et al., 1997), and Rc = 10⁻⁸ m²K/W as a swept assumption. Percolation and contact effects then emerge from the geometry without fitting. Three periodic solves, driven along x, y and z, give the full wall conductivity tensor; several random walls (2 in quick mode, 8 in full mode) give a low–likely–high band. The B-series inputs are sheets; the C2.5 slurry on the CNT-treated plies (about 56 wt% CNT in the dry solids) uses the same machinery with rods.

3.2.4 Numerical solution and computing hardware

Rungs 3 and 4 share one solver. The temperature is written as T = θ − Gx, with θ periodic and an imposed drop of 1 K per cell along the driven axis. Heat balance in every cell gives a symmetric positive semi-definite system Aθ = b, which is solved by the preconditioned conjugate gradient method (Hestenes & Stiefel, 1952) without ever assembling the matrix: applying A needs only array shifts (each neighbour is one roll of the array). The preconditioner is the same periodic grid filled with a uniform reference medium; its matrix is diagonal in Fourier space and is inverted exactly by two FFTs, the idea behind FFT-based homogenisation (Moulinec & Suquet, 1998). With it, the iteration count depends on the conductivity contrast rather than on the grid size: 264–284 iterations for the wall at a contrast of about 1500, and 67–95 for the honeycomb. Iterations stop at a relative residual of 10⁻⁸, and every solve checks that the same heat crosses every plane normal to the drive.

The same code runs on CPU through NumPy or on GPU through CuPy (Okuta et al., 2017), in double precision. Its memory need is about 112 bytes per voxel per solve (face conductances, CG vectors, temporaries and FFT buffers). Figure 3.7 translates this into hardware. The full-mode wall (5 nm voxels, 500 × 500 × 54) needs about 1.5 GB and fits on any current GPU; halving the voxel size to check convergence needs about 12 GB; halving again would need about 97 GB, beyond a single 80 GB card. The GPU is therefore needed for throughput, many seeds × three drives × sensitivity points, and for the convergence study, not for one large solve. Because a stencil-plus-FFT solver moves much more data than it computes, the relevant hardware figures are memory bandwidth and double-precision rate: data-centre GPUs such as the A100 and H100 provide several times the bandwidth of consumer cards and full-rate FP64 (NVIDIA, 2020; NVIDIA, 2022), whereas consumer GPUs execute FP64 at a small fraction of their FP32 rate. Colab sessions are not guaranteed a GPU type and are time-limited, so the runs are designed to be interrupted: fixed seeds, one result row saved per solve, and the GPU type, library versions and wall time logged with every result.

3.2.5 Gas conduction

Fast method: Knudsen formula. The conductivity of free air, kair(T), is interpolated from the property table of Bergman et al. (2011) (26.3 mW/m·K at 300 K). The mean free path follows from kinetic theory with an effective molecular diameter dg = 3.72 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, and the Knudsen-reduced conductivity from Kaganer (1969) and Zeng et al. (1995):

λ = kBT / (√2 π dg2 p),   Kn = λ/d,   kgas = kair / (1 + 2βKKn),   βK = [(2 − α)/α]·[2γ/(γ + 1)]/Pr(3.8)

With α = 1, γ = 1.4 and Pr = 0.71, βK = 1.643. At 300 K and 1 atm λ = 66.5 nm, Kn = 0.0022 for 30 µm pores, and the reduction is below 1 %. In the thin-wall limit the air fills a fraction φ of the cross-section in both directions, so the gas term is φkgas.

Detailed method 1: local Knudsen number. The pore size seen by the gas varies inside a real pore: it is smaller in corners and in cracks. The local pore size is taken as the diameter of the largest disc that fits in the pore and covers the pixel, the local-thickness transform of Hildebrand and Rüegsegger (1997), computed with Euclidean distance transforms on a picture padded with its periodic neighbours. Applying the Knudsen formula pixel by pixel gives a map of kgas, whose area mean is the gas term; Rung 2 can use the map directly.

Detailed method 2: Monte Carlo chords. Virtual molecules start at random points in the pores and fly in random three-dimensional directions; each flight is traced both ways to the first wall, giving a chord length ℓ. A random point lies more often on a long chord than on a short one, and the harmonic mean ℓ̄ = 1/⟨1/ℓ⟩ removes this bias, recovering Cauchy's mean chord 4V/S. Two parallel plates a gap d apart have ℓ̄ = 2d, so the equivalent gap is deff = ℓ̄/2 and kgas = kair/(1 + 2βKλ/deff). This deviates deliberately from the original brief, which proposed scaling kair by the ratio of wall-limited to free mean free paths: tested on parallel plates, that version disagreed with the Knudsen formula by 26 % at Kn = 0.1 and by a factor of 5.5 at Kn = 10, because it contains no accommodation physics. The chord version reproduces the parallel-plate Knudsen result within Monte Carlo noise from Kn = 0.01 to 10.

Comparison. Figure 3.8 compares the three methods on the same structure from 1 atm down to 0.1 kPa. At 1 atm they agree within 1 % (26.11, 25.95 and 25.86 mW/m·K). As the pressure falls they separate: at 1 kPa the formula gives 15.1, the local method 14.3 and the Monte Carlo method 9.6 mW/m·K. The reason is what “pore size” means. A smooth hexagonal channel has a mean chord equal to d (4A/P), so it behaves like a plate gap of d/2 (12.7 µm here, including about 10 % from the pixel staircase), not d. The definition is irrelevant at atmospheric pressure but decides the result in vacuum or in nanoporous walls, and the report therefore states it explicitly.

3.2.6 Radiation

Fast method 1: opaque walls. Each pore is treated as a gap of width d between two large grey walls. Linearising the net exchange σ(T14 − T24)/(2/ε − 1) for a small temperature difference gives

krad = 4σT3 d ε/(2 − ε)(3.9)

which is 0.150 mW/m·K at the defaults (ε = 0.9, hypothetical), used in both directions as a first approximation.

Fast method 2: Rosseland diffusion. If the material absorbs and scatters strongly, radiation behaves as conduction with krad = 16n²σT³/(3eρbulk) (Hrubesh & Pekala, 1994; Modest, 2013), valid only when the optical thickness τ = eρbulkL is well above unity. With an assumed specific extinction e = 30 m²/kg and a 3 mm layer, τ = 1.8; the method is therefore outside its range at the defaults, and the tool prints a warning rather than a number to be trusted.

Detailed method: ray tracing. Photon bundles with diffuse directions are launched into slabs of the pixel picture ¼, ½, 1, 2 and 4 cells thick. In a pore a bundle flies straight; at a wall it is reflected with probability R, absorbed inside the wall with probability (1 − R)(1 − e−κwt), or transmitted. Every bundle ends transmitted, reflected or absorbed, and the three fractions sum to one for every slab. A Beer's-law fit of transmittance against slab thickness gives the extinction coefficient β of the structure (Figure 3.9); the radiative conductivity then follows from Rosseland (βL ≥ 3) or from the two-flux slab expression 4σT³L/(1 + ¾βL) otherwise (Randrianalisoa & Baillis, 2014). Rays are confined to the cross-section, absorption uses the normal-incidence path, and reflected bundles leave in a random direction; these shortcuts are stated in the code. With opaque walls (κw → ∞, R = 0.1) the method gives 0.144 mW/m·K against 0.150 from the opaque-wall formula. At the default, hypothetical optics it gives β = 1.77 × 10⁴ m⁻¹ and krad = 0.46 mW/m·K.

The infrared gap. The radiative term is below 1 % of the total, but its inputs are the least certain in the model. The planned reflectance measurement covers 300–2500 nm, whereas thermal emission at 300 K peaks near 2898/300 ≈ 9.7 µm (Wien's law). The planned data will therefore not fix the radiative inputs; that needs FTIR measurements or literature optical constants for BN, CNF and PMSQ in the thermal infrared. Until then the radiative term is reported as an order of magnitude.

3.2.7 Convection

Fast method: Rayleigh criterion. Buoyancy drives air motion; viscosity and thermal diffusion resist it. For one pore heated across its width,

Ra = gβTΔT d3/(να),   βT = 1/T,   ΔT = (dT/dx)·d(3.10)

with ν = 15.89 × 10⁻⁶ and α = 22.5 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s for air at 300 K (Bergman et al., 2011). A layer heated from below starts to convect at Ra ≈ 1708. At d = 30 µm and an assumed gradient of 1000 K/m, Ra ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁸, so kconv = 0 with the reason printed. Since Ra scales with d⁴, onset would need pores of about 12 mm at that gradient, or 3.7 mm at 10⁵ K/m (Figure 3.10), consistent with the millimetre threshold reported for foams (Skochdopole, 1961).

Detailed method: lattice Boltzmann. To show rather than assume this result, the air in a pore is simulated as a layer heated from below at the pore's Rayleigh number, with a D2Q9 lattice for the flow, a D2Q5 lattice for the temperature, Boussinesq buoyancy and bounce-back walls (Shan, 1997; Mohamad, 2011). The heat carried is expressed as Nu = 1 + ⟨vT⟩H/(αΔT) and kconv = (Nu − 1)φkgas. The layer uses periodic sides rather than the hexagonal pore; real side walls only delay onset, so this is conservative. The method is benchmarked against published Rayleigh–Bénard values (Section 4.1) before it is applied to the pore, where it returns Nu = 1.000000.

3.2.8 Implementation

The web tool is organised as rows and methods (Figure 3.11). Each heat path is a folder, and each method is one Python file that declares its name, level (fast or detailed), equation, source, cost and inputs, and implements a single function returning k∥, k⊥, the paths it covers and notes. A registry discovers the files, a state object runs them, remembers the last result of each row and adds the paths once, and a small standard-library HTTP server exposes a JSON interface to the browser. The browser page holds no physics and no labels: every word shown on hover (equation, source, cost, the status of every input) comes from the method files, so the documentation cannot drift from the code. A three-dimensional view shows the honeycomb or the defects picture, coloured with the field returned by the last detailed run.

The K_cond solver is a single Python file intended to be pasted into a Colab cell. It has three editable input blocks (wall, honeycomb, run options), each input annotated with unit, status and source, and runs in a quick CPU mode (small grids, two random walls, under a minute on two cores) or a full GPU mode. Both pieces of software live in the project repository (saul-gitman/FYP on GitHub) with their plans, physics notes and the record of every design decision, so that every number in this report can be regenerated.

3.2.10 Planned specimen model (semester two)

The experimental programme tests six laminate layouts (L1–L5 and the plain reference P) made from two fixed slurries, B10 for the BN surface layer and BN-treated plies and C2.5 for the CNT-treated plies. 4.2 Baseline breakdown at the reference structure

At the defaults the fast methods give k⊥ = 35.5 + 25.6 + 0.150 + 0 = 61.3 mW/m·K and k∥ = 71.0 + 25.6 + 0.150 + 0 = 96.8 mW/m·K, an anisotropy of 1.58 (Figure 4.1; full worked example in Appendix A). Pixel FV, which resolves solid and gas together, gives 59.3 and 95.9 mW/m·K (anisotropy 1.62). The breakdown answers the first question the project asked: the solid walls carry 58 % of the heat across and 73 % along the channels, the air carries almost all the rest, radiation contributes 0.2 %, and convection is absent. The wall conductivity is therefore the single largest lever in the model, and the only input whose uncertainty is large enough to change the conclusions.

4.3 Pore-size sweep

The first study requested by the supervisor varies the pore size with everything else fixed (Figure 4.2). In mode A the wall thickness is held at 0.27 µm, so larger pores mean a higher porosity: k⊥ falls from 222 mW/m·K at 5 µm to 31 mW/m·K at 500 µm, and the anisotropy from 1.90 to 1.07, because the solid term shrinks while the gas term approaches the free-air value. In mode B the pitch is fixed, so the walls thin as the pores grow, with a similar, steeper trend. Mode C fixes the porosity instead; it violates the meeting's rule that porosity follows from geometry and is kept only as a diagnostic. It is nevertheless the most informative curve: at constant porosity k⊥ changes only from 60.3 to 63.8 mW/m·K over two decades of pore size, through the small Knudsen and radiative terms. At atmospheric pressure pore size matters through porosity, not through size itself, which means a freezing-condition study that changes the pore size without changing the solids loading should expect a small thermal effect unless the walls change too.

4.4 Effect of structural defects

Real freeze-cast walls are not continuous. Cutting a gap into every wall of the ideal picture (Figure 4.3) lowers k⊥ by 23 % at 5 % crack length and by 34 % at 15 %, while k∥ drops only 4 % and 11 % and the porosity barely changes (0.9825 to 0.9834 at 5 %). Cracks interrupt the inclined walls that carry heat across the channels but leave the walls parallel to the channels intact, so the anisotropy rises from 1.62 to 2.03 at 5 % and 2.18 at 15 %. The combined default defects (10 % cell spread, 20 % wall-thickness variation, 5 % cracks) give 45.4 and 92.0 mW/m·K. This is the effect the formula cannot see, and it moves the model in the direction of the measurements. It also saturates: even heavily cracked straight channels do not approach the measured anisotropy of 5.8.

4.5 Wall model: first results

The wall RVE was run in quick mode for the B10 recipe (83 wt% BN, dense wall density 1.93 g/cm³): 80 × 80 × 14 voxels of 20 nm over a 1.6 µm patch, two random walls, 1.35 s per solve on two CPU cores. The along-wall conductivity is 61.4 W/m·K (57.1–65.7 between the two walls), and the through-wall value 1.98 W/m·K, inside the bounds set by perfect parallel (229 W/m·K) and series (1.44 W/m·K) arrangements. These numbers are not converged, because a 20 nm voxel does not resolve a 30 nm sheet, and they are reported as an order of magnitude. Placed in the honeycomb at true wall thickness, this wall would give k⊥ = 573 mW/m·K (535–612) and k∥ = 1115 mW/m·K: about ten times the reference values and twenty times the measured aerogels.

A one-at-a-time sensitivity study (Figure 4.4) shows where this answer comes from. The sheet-to-sheet contact resistance Rc, swept from 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁷ m²K/W, moves the wall conductivity from 119 to 22 W/m·K, a swing of 158 % of the baseline. The BN in-plane conductivity (100–550 W/m·K) swings it by 80 %, the tilt spread of the sheets (3–30°) by 56 %, the sheet diameter by 55 % and a wall porosity of 0–40 % by 55 %. The BN–binder resistance RK and the conductivities of CNF and PMSQ each change it by less than 4 %. In a BN-continuous wall, heat crosses from sheet to sheet, so what matters is how the sheets touch, how large and flat they are, and how much empty space separates them; the binder is almost irrelevant. These are therefore the measurements to make first: sheet size and thickness of the BN grade (datasheet or TEM), sheet alignment and wall porosity (SEM and a density check), with the contact resistance kept as a swept unknown.

Dry versus resin-infused layers. The same bounds show where the GPU is worth using (Figure 4.5). For a dry layer the wall-to-air contrast is about 150, the Hashin–Shtrikman bracket spans a factor of 2.3, and doubling the wall conductivity raises k⊥ by 58 %; the exact geometry and the exact wall value both matter. If epoxy fills the cells (assumed 0.2 W/m·K), the contrast falls to about 20, the bracket narrows to 14 %, and doubling the wall conductivity changes k⊥ by only 15 %, so closed forms are sufficient. Infusion also changes the function of the layer: a BN surface layer flooded with resin would conduct about 3.8 times more across and 2.8 times more along its channels. Whether the BN layer stays dry during infusion is therefore the single most important question for the thermal comparison of the specimens, and the mass gain on infusion should be recorded for every plate.

4.6 Model versus measurement

The model was not calibrated to any measurement, so its disagreements with the literature are informative. Figure 4.6 and Table 4.2 summarise three discrepancies, their candidate causes and the evidence that would resolve each.

Discrepancy 1: below the air floor. Transverse values of 16.2–23.5 mW/m·K reported for BNNS/polymer aerogels lie below the 25.6 mW/m·K that the air in a 98 %-porous honeycomb of 30 µm channels must carry at 1 atm, before any solid or radiative contribution. No model of air-filled micron channels can reach them by changing the wall conductivity, and tuning inputs to match would hide the cause. Three explanations are physically possible. (i) The walls contain nanopores: gas in pores of tens of nanometres is Knudsen-suppressed, and porous walls also conduct less. The density check of Section 2.3 supports this possibility, since the walls of this project's own recipe would be 1.93 g/cm³ if dense, while the reference wall density is 1.12 g/cm³. (ii) The channels are not continuous across the sample, so the gas path is longer than assumed. (iii) The transient plane source measurement underestimates the transverse value of a strongly anisotropic, highly porous sample (Gustafsson, 1991; Lou et al., 2023). The first can be tested with SEM of wall cross-sections and a porosity measurement, the third by measuring the project's own aerogels with a steady-state method. The CNF/BNNS/MTMS cryogel of Zhao et al. (2022), the published material closest to the B-series, reports 33.9 mW/m·K radially: above the floor and consistent with the physics of the model.

Discrepancy 2: anisotropy. The model gives 1.6 for ideal and up to 2.2 for heavily cracked straight channels, against 5.8 for BNNS/WPU (Chan et al., 2022). For the project's own constituent family the comparison is favourable: the CNF/BNNS/MTMS cryogel of Zhao et al. (2022) shows 1.83, inside the modelled range, so the gap belongs to the BNNS/WPU system rather than to the model. Two mechanisms not yet modelled could close the gap: transverse bridges and wall waviness, which would require a three-dimensional structure from SEM (for example by SliceGAN; (Kench & Cooper, 2021)), and BN sheets aligned in the walls, which raise the along-wall conductivity relative to the across-wall one. An underestimated transverse measurement (discrepancy 1) would also inflate the measured anisotropy.

Discrepancy 3: the wall conductivity. The ideal B10 wall gives about 61 W/m·K, fifteen times the 4.0 W/m·K back-calculated from Chan et al. Part of this gap is not a model error at all. As shown in Section 2.3, the porosity definition of that paper implies a skeleton density of 1.12 g/cm³ and hence a polymer-rich wall of roughly 8–16 wt% BNNS, whereas the B10 wall is 83 wt% BN; the two walls are different materials and the reference value is not a target for B10. The rest of the gap lies in the hypothetical microstructure inputs (contact resistance, sheet size and alignment, wall porosity) and in the unconverged quick grid. The planned analysis is to report which combinations of these inputs would bring the wall down to a given value, and to replace them with measurements, rather than to fit them.

4.7 Limitations

The present results carry the following limitations, each stated in the code where it applies. The web tool draws walls 4 pixels wide with a reduced solid fraction rather than at true thickness (removed by the GPU solver). Ray tracing is two-dimensional, and the wall optical properties, specific extinction, emissivity and accommodation coefficient are hypothetical. The lattice Boltzmann check uses a periodic layer rather than the hexagonal pore, and the air properties in the Rayleigh number are fixed at 300 K. The wall RVE results come from an unconverged quick grid with two random walls, and its sheet geometry, contact resistance and PMSQ properties are hypothetical. The heat-path decomposition neglects solid–gas coupling except where Pixel FV resolves both phases. Finally, the reference geometry is taken from the literature; the project's own pore size, wall thickness and channel direction will replace it once SEM images of the specimens are available.

6 Conclusions and Next Steps

The work to date supports five conclusions.

  1. A verified, two-level model of the conductivity of a dry freeze-cast honeycomb aerogel has been built. Every detailed method reproduces its formula in the ideal case, the solvers reproduce exact solutions to round-off, and the true-thickness solver matches an independent finite-element benchmark within 0.35 %.
  2. At the reference structure the solid walls carry 58 % of the heat across and 73 % along the channels; radiation contributes 0.2 % and convection none. The wall conductivity is therefore the decisive input, and at atmospheric pressure pore size acts through porosity rather than through size itself.
  3. Wall cracks lower the transverse conductivity by 23 % at 5 % crack length and raise the anisotropy to about 2, but straight-channel air-filled models cannot reach the sub-air transverse values or the anisotropy of 5.8 reported for BNNS/polymer aerogels, most plausibly because of nanoporous walls, three-dimensional channel features or the transverse test method. For the project's own constituent family the model is consistent: a CNF/BNNS/MTMS cryogel shows an anisotropy of 1.83 and a transverse value above the air floor. Because the freezing set-up grows channels through the laminate thickness, the specimens' through-thickness value corresponds to the layers' high direction.
  4. The B-series recipes produce BN-continuous walls (62–91 vol% BN), outside the range of dilute mixture models. A first voxel model of the B10 wall gives about 61 W/m·K, controlled mainly by sheet-to-sheet contacts, BN conductivity, sheet alignment and wall porosity; the 4 W/m·K reference from the literature describes a polymer-rich wall and is not a target for B10.
  5. The measurements that would most reduce the uncertainty are SEM of the specimens (pore size, wall thickness, channel direction, wall porosity), the sheet dimensions of the BN grade, the resin fill fraction after infusion, and infrared optical properties of the walls.

Next steps. In January the GPU solver will be run in full mode with a voxel-halving convergence study, and the specimen model will be built for L1–L5 and P with calibration on P only. In February the uncertainty of the chain will be propagated with Sobol analysis and SEM-based inputs introduced. In March the composition sweep matching the Semester-2 screening groups (B0–B20, C0–C7.5) will be run and the code frozen ahead of the final report.
